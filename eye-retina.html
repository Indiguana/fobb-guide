2. From pinhole to eye

We see objects because they reflect light. The problem: light reflects in all directions, so every point on a wall receives light from every point of the object. It all mixes → a blur, not an image.

Ibn al-Haytham (965–1040) worked out the fix: let only one ray from each point through. A camera obscura — light passing through a small hole into a dark space — forms a clear, inverted image. Drag the hole size and watch the tradeoff:

Camera obscura — the aperture tradeoff
What to notice: a tiny hole gives a sharp but dim image; a big hole is bright but blurry, because each object point now spreads over a patch. A lens breaks the tradeoff — it bends the whole bundle of rays back to one point, so you get a big hole and a sharp image. Brightness ∝ hole area, blur ∝ hole width (simple ray geometry).
Pinhole's three problems
  1. Dim
  2. Narrow field of view
  3. Fixed focus — can't adjust for near vs far
(It was used for art — and some deemed that mystical, or cheating.)
The eye's solutions
  • Bigger hole, letting more light in
  • Pupil dilates and contracts to adjust light
  • Wide field of view with 2 eyes
  • Cornea bends a lot of light into the eye
  • Accommodation: the lens changes shape to adjust focus
Accommodation demo (Monroe / Einstein) A hybrid image that looks like Einstein up close and Marilyn Monroe from far away. Focus changes the type of information processed: focused / close-up → fine detail (Einstein); unfocused / far → coarse information (Monroe). This coarse-vs-fine split returns in the magno/parvo pathways (Ch. 10).

Darwin admitted that the eye — with its "inimitable contrivances for adjusting the focus to different distances, for admitting different amounts of light" — made natural selection seem "absurd in the highest degree" (he went on to explain how it could evolve anyway).

Where rods and cones are (schematic)
Schematic reconstruction of the lecture's density plot — shapes are right, heights are not to scale. What to notice: cones spike at the fovea; rods are absent there and dominate the periphery; nothing is at the blind spot (where the optic nerve leaves), on the nasal side.
Other species sit at different points on the rod–cone spectrum Mantis shrimp: 12 colour-sensitive photoreceptors (humans: 3), can detect ultraviolet, no rods. Cats: lots of rods, few cones — good at seeing in the dark.

Averted vision

If rods are in the periphery and rods handle dim light, then to see a faint star you should look next to it. That's averted vision — used in astronomy and taught in the U.S. Army field manual: look about 10 degrees above, below or to the side of an object at night.

4. The blind spot and perceptual completion

Where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no photoreceptors: a blind spot in each eye. Find yours:

Blind-spot test
What to notice: at some distance the pink X vanishes — and you don't see a hole. You see the orange bar running straight through. Your brain filled the gap with its best guess.

Why don't you normally notice it? Two reasons:

  1. Two eyes cover for each other — each eye's blind spot falls on a part of the scene the other eye can see.
  2. Perceptual completion — the brain "fills in" gaps by making an educated guess. The brain is always making predictions.

5. Visual adaptation: why you don't see your blood vessels

Blood vessels lie in front of your retina. They should cast shadows on your photoreceptors all day. Why don't you see them? Because they're stationary relative to the photoreceptors, and the brain filters out constant, unchanging stimuli.

Visual adaptation The brain filters out unchanging stimuli, and is more sensitive to changes (like motion).
Troxler fading — stare at the dot for 20 seconds
What to notice: keep your eyes perfectly still and the blobs fade into the background. Blink or move your eyes and they snap back — the change resets adaptation. Blurry, peripheral, unchanging stimuli are exactly what the system filters out, same as your retinal blood vessels.

6. The visual pathway — and what damage does

The trick to every lesion question: the left visual field goes to the right hemisphere. Light from the left side of the world lands on the right half of each retina (the lens flips it). The fibres from the inner (nasal) half of each retina cross at the optic chiasm; the outer (temporal) fibres don't. After the chiasm, each optic tract carries one half of the visual world from both eyes.

The rule behind the table Before the chiasm (retina, optic nerve) → damage affects one eye. At the chiasm → the crossing (nasal) fibres are hit → both outer fields. 7. Check yourself

A stroke destroys the left primary visual cortex. The patient loses…
Why: After the chiasm, each hemisphere handles the opposite visual field from both eyes. Left V1 → right field lost (contralateral homonymous hemianopia). "All vision in the left eye" would need damage before the chiasm.
A pituitary tumour presses on the optic chiasm from below. Expected deficit?
Why: The chiasm is where nasal fibres cross; nasal retina sees the temporal (outer) field. Hit them and both outer fields go: bitemporal hemianopia.
You're trying to spot a dim star. You should…
Why: The fovea is packed with cones, which need bright light. Rods — scotopic, low-light — are in the periphery. That's averted vision.
You don't see the shadows of your retinal blood vessels because of…
Why: The key word is unchanging. Perceptual completion is the blind-spot answer (filling a gap); adaptation is the blood-vessel answer (filtering a constant).
Rhodopsin is to ___ as photopsin is to ___.
Why: Rhodopsin → rods → dim light. Photopsins → cones → bright light and colour. The fovea/periphery option is the right association backwards.
