1. Methods

Short answerBenefits and limitations of behavior research (L7 study guide Q1)

Give three benefits and three limitations, each with an example from the face lectures.

Benefits
  1. Characterizes representations — orientation-specific vs invariant (Dutch politicians).
  2. Dissociates representations — faces vs objects (inversion effect).
  3. Cheap and low-tech.
Limitations
  1. Sparse, limited data — one response per trial.
  2. Hard to see fine-grained stages of processing — reaction time lumps perception, decision and motor planning together.
  3. No relation to the brain.
Short answerBenefits and limitations of patient studies (L7 study guide Q2)

Use prosopagnosia and patient CK.

Benefits

Evidence that a region is necessary (a causal clue) — an FFA lesion abolishes face recognition. Double dissociations (prosopagnosia vs CK) show two processes are distinct.

Limitations
  1. Hard to find patients with the "right" lesion.
  2. Lesions are messy — affect multiple functions.
  3. Chronic lesions → the brain reorganizes during recovery; effects may reflect recovery, not the lesion.
  4. Not a direct manipulation — can't turn a region off and back on.
DesignPick the method

For each question, name the best method and the one limitation that matters most: (a) Is there a region that responds more to tools than to animals? (b) Does the tool response start before or after 200 ms? (c) Is that region necessary for naming tools?

(a) Where?

fMRI — best whole-brain spatial resolution. Key limitation: correlational (can't show causality); also slow and indirect (BOLD).

(b) When?

EEG/MEG (non-invasive, ms timing) — but can't localize to the region. If you need both where and when: ECoG/sEEG, at the cost of rare, invasive, limited-coverage recordings.

(c) Necessary?

Perturbation: patients with lesions there (messy, recovery), or direct electrical stimulation via ECoG (strong causal evidence; can't separate the region from its connections; special patient group).

The takeaway

Where → fMRI; when → EEG/MEG; where + when → ECoG/sEEG; causal → patients/stimulation. And the big-picture sentence: no single method gives the full picture — robust claims come from convergence.

A researcher shows FFA activity correlates with face recognition in fMRI, EEG and sEEG. What is still missing for a causal claim?
Why: Every one of those is a recording method. To test X → Y you must disrupt X and watch Y — patients or stimulation.

What can you conclude, and which task is useless?

Step 1 — What's being asked?

Classify the pattern (none / single / double dissociation) and spot any task that lacks sensitivity.

Step 2 — Tools vs animals

Group 1: tools impaired, animals spared. Group 2: animals impaired, tools spared. Opposite patterns → double dissociation between tool and animal naming, supporting separate (parallel) processes, and ruling out general impairment and task difficulty.

Step 3 — Colours

Everyone is at 97–99%: a ceiling effect. You can't conclude colour naming is unaffected — the task can't detect a deficit.

ExplainWhy a double dissociation supports the parallel model

Serial: Rhythm → Melody → Music. Parallel: Rhythm → Music and Melody → Music. The RH group shows melody impaired / rhythm spared; the LH group shows rhythm impaired / melody spared. Explain which result does the work.

Answer

Melody-impaired / rhythm-spared (RH) fits both models — break the melody stage in either and only melody fails. Rhythm-impaired / melody-spared (LH) is impossible under the serial model — melody depends on rhythm's output. So the LH result is what rules out serial and favours parallel.

3. Frameworks

ApplyMarr + Tinbergen for depth perception

Give all three Marr levels and all four Tinbergen questions for depth perception.

Marr

Computational: reach and grasp accurately, navigate safely, judge approaching objects. Algorithmic: combine pictorial cues (occlusion, perspective, height, known size, texture, atmosphere, shadows) with non-pictorial cues (disparity, parallax, vergence, accommodation), choosing the interpretation that fits the most cues. Implementation: V1–V5; disparity processed from V1 to V5/MT.

Tinbergen

Function: same as computational. Mechanism: algorithm + implementation above. Development: how depth perception emerges in infancy — e.g. stereopsis needs both eyes aligned during the critical period (strabismus → amblyopia). Evolution: predators (forward-facing eyes, disparity) vs prey (wide field of view); the praying mantis's head-swaying for motion parallax.

"The cerebellum generates sensory predictions" is an answer at which Marr level?
Why: The lecture explicitly frames it at the algorithmic level — the computation the cerebellum performs. "The cerebellum" names hardware, but the claim is about the computation.

4. Clinical reasoning

CaseThree patients, one circuit

Patient 1: slow, small movements, resting tremor. Patient 2: involuntary dance-like movements, cognitive decline, family history. Patient 3: a Parkinson's patient on high-dose medication for eight years, now with writhing involuntary movements. For each: diagnosis, pathway, and chain of effects.

Patient 1

Parkinson's. Dopamine loss (substantia nigra) → striatum less active → less inhibition of GPi → more inhibition of thalamus → less excitation of cortex. Go pathway.

Patient 2

Huntington's (genetic). Striatal loss in the no-go pathway → less inhibition of GPe → more inhibition of STN → less excitation of GPi → less inhibition of thalamus → chorea. Plus dementia.

Patient 3

L-dopa-induced dyskinesia: unnatural dopamine influx + receptor sensitization → small amounts of dopamine now trigger involuntary movement. Option: DBS of GPi, which releases the thalamus directly without extra dopamine.

CaseWhere is the lesion?

(a) Loses the left half of the visual field in both eyes. (b) Loses the outer half of each eye's field. (c) Blind in the right eye only. (d) Fluent nonsense speech, poor comprehension. (e) Can't recognize her husband's face but recognizes his voice and names objects fine. (f) Sees moving cars as a series of snapshots.

Answers

(a) Right optic tract / LGN / radiation / V1 — after the chiasm (contralateral homonymous hemianopia). (b) Optic chiasm (bitemporal hemianopia). (c) Right optic nerve, before the chiasm. (d) Wernicke's area (Wernicke's aphasia). (e) FFA — prosopagnosia. (f) MT/V5 — akinetopsia (patient LM).

CaseStroke or trauma?

(a) A retired linebacker with depression, explosive temper and memory complaints. (b) A cyclist in an immediate coma after a crash with a violent head twist. (c) Sudden worst-ever headache, vomiting, collapse. (d) A clot in the anterior cerebral artery.

Answers

(a) CTE stage 2 — repeated head trauma, frontal tau. (b) Diffuse axonal injury — rotational shear. (c) Hemorrhagic stroke from a ruptured aneurysm (treat unruptured ones by clipping or coiling). (d) Ischemic stroke in frontal + parietal territory.

5. Vision mechanisms

ExplainHermann grid in three sentences

Explain the grey dots using ON-center, OFF-surround ganglion cells at an intersection vs a corridor.

Model answer

Both cells' centers sit on white and are equally excited. At an intersection, white extends in four directions, so more light hits the OFF-surround than in a corridor, where white runs in only two. More surround inhibition → weaker response → the intersection is seen as darker, a grey dot.

ExplainThree adaptation effects, one mechanism

Connect: not seeing your retinal blood vessels; tilt aftereffect; waterfall (motion) aftereffect.

Model answer

All three come from neurons reducing their response to sustained stimulation. Blood vessels: unchanging input is filtered out (visual adaptation). Tilt: fatigued orientation-tuned simple cells bias the population away from the adapting tilt. Motion: fatigued direction-tuned MT cells let the opposite direction dominate, so a static scene seems to move the opposite way. The brain codes change and balance, not absolute input.

Order the receptive fields from simplest to most complex.
Why: Itself → center–surround → center–surround → oriented lines/edges.
In the Kanizsa figure you perceive an illusory triangle on top and whole discs continuing behind it. Which is which?
Why: Modal = you actually see an illusory surface. Amodal = you infer an occluded part without seeing it.
Cones are to ___ as rods are to ___.
Why: Every other option is reversed. (Cones feed parvo more, rods feed magno more — so "magno, parvo" is the wrong order too.)

6. Foundations

ExplainThree drug mechanisms

Explain how each raises effective neurotransmitter signalling: SSRIs, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors.

Answer

SSRIs: block serotonin reuptake → more serotonin stays in the synapse. Dopamine agonists: mimic dopamine and bind its receptors directly. MAO-B inhibitors: block an enzyme that breaks dopamine down → it lasts longer.

If action potentials are all-or-none, a neuron signals a stronger stimulus by…
Why: Frequency coding.
Output from a cortical column back to the thalamus leaves from layers…
Why: 4 = input, 1–3 = cortex ↔ cortex, 5–6 = output (the Lecture 3 correction).
CSF circulates beneath which meninx?
Why: The subarachnoid space, between arachnoid and pia.
