Neuropsychology & dissociations

How studying damaged brains links a mental process to a brain region — why you need controls, why ceiling and floor effects hide deficits, and exactly what a double dissociation buys you that a single one can't.

1. The logic of a neuropsychological experiment

Cognitive neuroscientists bridge Marr's algorithmic and implementation levels: they link representations and processes to the tissue that runs them. You can't ethically switch off part of a healthy person's brain, but nature sometimes does it for you — stroke, tumour, injury.

Definition Neuropsychological experiment — a behavioral experiment with patients who have neurological disorders, used to relate brain and behavior: how is behavior disrupted when a particular part of the brain is damaged? How is a cognitive process linked to a particular area?

The lecture's two conclusions from round 1:

  1. Unclear support: impaired on folk songs, but the jingles and pop results are inconclusive (no sensitivity), so we can't claim a general music deficit.
  2. Limited explanatory depth: even if true, it links "music" to a region without saying which mental process is involved.

3. Round 2: add a cognitive model → single dissociation

Fix the second problem with a model. Music perception = two processes:

  • Rhythm recognition — extracting the timing and beat.
  • Melody recognition — extracting the sequence and relationship of pitches.

New hypothesis: the right hemisphere is essential for melody recognition. Rhythm becomes the control task. Which result supports it?

RH patients vs controls. Rhythm (control task) / Melody (experimental task):
Why: Melody impaired while rhythm is spared points specifically at melody. (4) looks like support but can't be told apart from general brain damage — the patients are just worse at everything. (3) supports the opposite claim. (1) means no deficit.
Definition Single dissociation — a brain lesion impairs one cognitive process while sparing another, suggesting the two are at least partly distinct. Uses one patient group.

Three weaknesses of a single dissociation

WeaknessWhat it means here
1. Task sensitivityMaybe the melody task is just harder — more sensitive to any damage — than the rhythm task.
2. General impairmentMaybe poor melody performance is a general consequence of brain damage, not damage to a specific process.
3. Model ambiguityCan't tell whether the two processes are serial (one after the other) or parallel (independent).

Serial vs parallel — why one patient group can't decide

Two models of music perception
What to notice: in the serial model, melody sits downstream of rhythm. Break melody → only melody fails; that's what both models predict. Break rhythm → in the serial model melody loses its input and fails too. So the telling case is the one the RH group didn't show: rhythm impaired, melody spared.
The step the slides skip Why does a double dissociation "support the parallel model"? Because the second group (rhythm broken, melody fine) is impossible under the serial model — if melody needed rhythm's output, it couldn't survive rhythm's loss. The RH result alone (melody broken, rhythm fine) is compatible with both models, which is the "model ambiguity" weakness.

5. Check yourself

Patients with frontal damage score 99% on a memory test; controls score 100%. The researcher concludes frontal cortex isn't involved in memory. The biggest problem is…
Why: Everyone is near maximum, so a real deficit couldn't show up. There is a control group. Floor would be everyone near zero.
Group A is impaired on both task X and task Y. What can you conclude about X and Y?
Why: No dissociation at all — no task was spared. Could be a shared process, a serial dependency, or just general brain damage.
Under a serial model Rhythm → Melody → Music, which patient pattern should be impossible?
Why: Melody depends on rhythm's output in the serial model; lose rhythm and melody should fail too. Finding this pattern (the LH group) is what supports the parallel model.
Try itDesign a double dissociation for faces vs objects

A patient with a lesion near the fusiform gyrus can't recognize his wife's face but names the shrine behind a sumo wrestler instantly. Someone argues: "faces are just harder objects; any damage hurts them first." What evidence would defeat that argument?

Step 1 — Which weakness is the critic using?

Task sensitivity: the face task might just be harder.

Step 2 — What would defeat it?

A patient with the opposite pattern: object recognition impaired, face recognition intact. If faces were just harder objects, anyone bad at objects should be even worse at faces.

Answer & the takeaway

Patient CK (object agnosia) is exactly that — Chapter 8. Prosopagnosia + object agnosia = double dissociation → faces and objects are distinct processes. The pattern you're practicing: when someone says "that task is just harder", find the mirror-image patient.
