Imaging & perturbing the brain, told through faces

Every method in the course — behavior, fMRI, EEG/MEG, ECoG, sEEG, patient studies, electrical stimulation — introduced by the question about faces it can answer, with the benefits and limitations you need to know cold.

2. Behavior: how are faces represented?

Two hypotheses: (a) we hold one orientation-invariant representation of each face, or (b) we memorize many orientation-specific views (which needs a lot of experience).

Try itThe Dutch-politician demo — reconstruct the logic

A collage shows many photos of Dutch politicians from different angles and lighting. Two groups count how many distinct people are shown: people familiar with Dutch politicians and people who aren't. What does each hypothesis predict?

Step 1 — What would "orientation-invariant" predict?

If everyone extracts one view-independent representation, experience shouldn't matter much — both groups equally good.

Step 2 — And "orientation-specific"?

If recognition needs stored templates for each view, the familiar group — who have seen these faces from many angles — should be much better.

Answer & the takeaway

Correct answer: 2 people. Unfamiliar viewers typically say 7. Face recognition benefits hugely from experience → more exposure = more orientation-specific templates = better recognition. The method lesson: behavioral experiments characterize representations by making the hypotheses predict different performance.

The face inversion effect

Turning an image upside down disrupts face recognition much more than object (e.g. house) recognition. That disproportionate cost suggests faces are processed by specialized mechanisms. Try it — one version of this face has been tampered with:

Which face is distorted? (flip to find out)
What to notice: upside down, the two faces look nearly the same; upright, one is obviously grotesque. Inverted, you read features one at a time and each looks fine; upright, faces are processed as a configured whole, so the wrong relationships jump out. going deeper This particular version is the "Thatcher illusion", a well-known cousin of the lecture's demo.
Behavior — benefits
  • Characterizes representations (orientation-specific vs invariant)
  • Dissociates representations (face vs object)
  • Cheap and low-tech
Behavior — limitations
  • Sparse, limited data (one response per trial)
  • Hard to tell the fine-grained stages of processing
  • No relation to the brain

3. fMRI: is there a face region?

Hypothesis: some place in the brain responds more to faces than to objects. Method: fMRI. It measures the BOLD (Blood Oxygenation Level–Dependent) signal — changes in the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated hemoglobin. Active neurons pull in oxygen-rich blood; the scanner sees the blood, not the neurons.

Finding: the fusiform face area (FFA) on the underside of the temporal lobe.

  • Responds to faces more than anything else tested
  • Found in a similar region in every subject
  • Shows a face-inversion effect for Mooney faces (two-tone faces) — same stimuli upright vs inverted, different response, so it isn't low-level image features
Try it"Any other hypotheses that can account for these data?"

The FFA lights up more for faces than for objects. List alternative explanations, then say how the lecture ruled them out.

Alternatives

(1) It responds to any body part. (2) It responds to anything curvy. (3) Faces are more interesting, so it responds to anything attended.

How they were ruled out

Show other body parts (hands), curvy non-face objects, and make people attend to everything equally with a 1-back task: watch a stream of stimuli and press when the current one matches the one immediately before. Now every stimulus is attended. FFA still prefers faces.

Takeaway

A difference between two conditions has many possible causes; good design adds control conditions that each kill one alternative. Attention confounds are killed by giving a task that forces equal attention.

fMRI — benefits The best spatial resolution for measuring activity across the whole brain.
fMRI — limitations
  • How BOLD relates to neural activity is unclear and indirect
  • Low temporal resolution: lags neural activity by 2–3 s
  • ~1 mm spatial resolution — can't resolve cortical layers (~0.5 mm)
  • Susceptibility artifact near ears and sinuses
  • Expensive (about 600 dollars an hour)
  • Can't tell if a region plays a causal role

4. EEG, MEG, ECoG, sEEG: how fast?

Why not just use reaction times? Because an RT bundles too much: processing might have started earlier, and the RT also contains motor planning and decision making (e.g. confidence). You need to watch the brain directly, with millisecond precision.

Fix the study guide The L6 study guide calls sEEG "single neuron EEG". The slides' name is stereoelectroencephalography — depth electrodes placed in 3D ("stereo"). The "single neuron" part is what it can record, not what the letters stand for.
The number to remember EEG / MEG / ECoG / single-neuron recording all converge: faces are detected 150–170 ms after they appear.

6. Causality: patients and stimulation

Every recording method above shares one limitation: it can't tell if a region plays a causal role. To test whether X causes Y, you have to mess with X and see whether Y breaks.

Patient studies

Prosopagnosia Cannot recognize familiar faces. Face recognition is a distribution; ~2% of the population has prosopagnosia. A patient with an FFA lesion couldn't recognize his wife — but shown a photo of sumo champion Akebono in front of the Meiji shrine, he failed on the face and immediately named the shrine.
  • Impairs face recognition, not face detection
  • Voice recognition intact (can identify people that way)
  • Object recognition intact
= single dissociation.
Object agnosia — patient CK 33-year-old, closed head injury. Severely impaired visual object recognition, normal face recognition.
  • Not memory? Tactile object recognition is normal → it's a visual agnosia.
  • Not lost knowledge? Asked to define a pipe: "a short, hollow object, larger on one end, 120° angle, for leisurely smoking using tobacco."
Together with prosopagnosia = double dissociation: faces and objects are distinct processes.
Why CK is necessary Prosopagnosia alone leaves the "task sensitivity" loophole from Ch. 5: maybe faces are just harder objects. CK closes it — if faces were just harder objects, someone who fails on objects couldn't be fine on faces. And the two "but how do you know…" checks (touch, definitions) are there to rule out that CK's problem is memory or knowledge rather than visual recognition.
Patient studies — benefits Some evidence that FFA is necessary for face recognition.
Patient studies — limitations
  • Hard to find patients with the "right" lesion
  • Lesions are messy — affect multiple functions
  • Chronic lesions: the brain reorganizes during recovery, so effects may reflect recovery rather than the lesion
  • Not a direct manipulation — you can't turn the area off then on

Direct electrical stimulation

Neurosurgeons treating seizures need to find the seizure's origin and check whether it's safe to operate (e.g. preserve language). They electrically stimulate the cortex. ECoG electrodes can both record and stimulate — turning an area off, then back on. Stimulating FFA electrodes distorts the faces patients see, establishing a causal role for FFA.

Stimulation — benefits Strong evidence that FFA is causally involved in face recognition — a real on/off manipulation.
Stimulation — limitations
  • Can't tell if disruption is due to FFA specifically or FFA's connections with other areas
  • Special patient group (seizure patients) who may have a propensity for the function you're studying
Big takeaway No single method gives the full picture. Robust results come from convergence across methods: behavior (inversion effect, templates) + imaging (FFA selectivity) + timing (150–170 ms) + patients and stimulation (causality). Embrace the brain's complexity.

7. Check yourself

You want to know where in the whole brain a task activates, non-invasively. Best method?
Why: fMRI has the best spatial resolution for the whole brain. ECoG has better resolution but is invasive and covers only a patch. EEG is great at when, lousy at where.
Which method, on its own, can establish that FFA causes face recognition?
Why: Only perturbation (turning a region off/on) tests causality. All recording methods — however precise — show correlation. sEEG recording is tempting because of its precision, but precision isn't causation.
Why can't reaction time alone tell you how fast faces are detected?
Why: The lecture's three reasons. Spatial resolution isn't the issue for a timing question.
Patient CK recognizes objects normally by touch. This rules out that his deficit is…
Why: If he'd lost object memory, touch would fail too. Intact touch pins the deficit to vision — visual object agnosia.
A limitation shared by patient studies with chronic lesions but not by electrical stimulation is…
Why: Stimulation is acute (on, then off), so recovery can't reshape the result. "Region vs its connections" is actually a stimulation limitation.
